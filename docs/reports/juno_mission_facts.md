## Q1 — Mission timeline

- **Launch:** August 5, 2011 (S1, S13 agree; not independently re-verified against a primary NASA page in this pass — status: verified via converging secondary sources, not a direct primary-page quote).
- **Jupiter Orbit Insertion (JOI):** July 4, 2016 (US date; JPL/NASA press language), = July 5, 2016 UTC per some sources. S1: JOI listed as "July 4, 2016." VERIFIED (converging sources).
- **Perijove 1 (PJ1):** August 27, 2016. VERIFIED (converging WebSearch sources incl. NASA-sourced snippet "Juno passed about 2,600 miles (4,200 kilometers) above Jupiter's cloud tops, marking Perijove 1").
- **Orbital period, start:** ~53-day (53.5-day) polar orbit maintained through most of prime + first extended mission. S3 (science.nasa.gov): *"During most of its mission, NASA's Juno spacecraft maintained 53-day polar orbits around Jupiter."* VERIFIED.
  - Note: the ORIGINAL pre-launch plan called for 14-day orbits (a period-reduction burn after JOI), but that burn was cancelled after an engine-valve issue was found, so Juno stayed in the ~53-day orbit for the whole prime + first extended mission. S12 (Spaceflight Now, 2018): *"Scientists planned to have Juno complete 32 of the 14-day science orbits through February of this year, when its prime mission was scheduled to be over."* VERIFIED that the 14-day plan existed and was superseded.
- **Ganymede flyby → 43-day orbit:** June 7, 2021 (PJ34). S1: *"Ganymede flyby: 'June 7, 2021 (PJ34), which reduces the orbital period from about 53 days to 43 days'"*. VERIFIED (S1, corroborated by S13's "June 8, 2021" — one-day discrepancy between sources, likely UTC/local date difference, UNVERIFIED which is exact).
- **Europa flyby → 38-day orbit:** September 29, 2022 (PJ45). S1: *"Europa flyby: 'Sept. 29, 2022 (PJ45), reducing the orbital period further to 38 days'"*. VERIFIED.
- **Io flybys → 33-day orbit:** December 30, 2023 (PJ57) and February 3, 2024 (PJ58) combined. S1: *"Io flybys: 'Dec. 30, 2023 (PJ57), and Feb. 3, 2024 (PJ58), combine to reduce the orbital period to 33 days'"*. VERIFIED. (S3 corroborates: *"culminating in 33-day orbits as of February 2024."*)
- **Prime mission end:** Originally planned for February 2018 (32 orbits at the pre-launch-planned 14-day cadence). S12: as quoted above. First no-cost extension (approved June 2018) funded operations "through fiscal year 2022," with *"Mission operations ... set to end in July 2021, with continued data analysis through 2022"* (S12). VERIFIED, though terminology is inconsistently used across NASA materials — some later pages loosely call the whole 2016–2021 span the "prime mission." Flag: mildly UNVERIFIED which single date NASA now treats as canonical "prime mission end."
- **Extended mission (2nd) end date, as approved:** September 2025 — specifically "through September 2025, or its end of life (whichever comes first)" per S10, the January 8, 2021 JPL announcement *"NASA Extends Exploration for Two Planetary Science Missions."* Quote: *"Extended through September 2025, or its end of life (whichever comes first)."* VERIFIED. This extension added 42 orbits including Ganymede/Europa/Io flybys and ring exploration (S1).

## Q2 — Current status (2025–2026)

- The formally-approved second extension expired **September 30, 2025**, coinciding with the start of a U.S. government shutdown on October 1, 2025. S8 (Fox Weather, published Oct 8, 2025): *"The spacecraft is 'likely no longer operating' following the end of its mission extension during the U.S. government shutdown that began October 1, 2025."* Same article: proposed White House FY2026 budget put Juno at risk of elimination under a ~47% NASA science cut. VERIFIED as reported by this outlet; status of spacecraft itself (operating vs. not) was explicitly uncertain in the source.
- Congress pushed back: House appropriators' FY2026 report language explicitly directed continuation of Juno (along with Chandra, New Horizons, etc.) rather than cancellation, per WebSearch synthesis of Planetary Society / SpaceNews coverage (UNVERIFIED by direct primary-doc fetch in this pass — based on WebSearch snippets, not a page I fetched and read directly; treat as probably-true but not independently quote-verified).
- **As of March 24, 2026**, AIAA reported Juno as operating in good health but with no confirmed further NASA extension decision: S9 quote: *"Juno remains in good health, but NASA officials have not said if they will approve another extension for the mission."* VERIFIED (direct fetch).
- **As of May 1, 2026**, Juno actively performed a close flyby/imaging pass of the small moon Thebe (~5,000 km distance), reported May 5, 2026 by phys.org (S11) and by JPL/NASA Science photojournal pages found in search (PIA26751). This confirms the spacecraft was still operating and returning new data in May 2026. VERIFIED (S11 direct fetch; JPL PIA26751 page found via search but not independently re-fetched — mark as high-confidence but not double-quote-verified).
- **Most recent confirmed perijove number/date:** SwRI's own perijove table (S2) is authoritative in principle but two separate WebFetch extraction passes of the SAME page returned inconsistent last rows — one pass said the table's final row was **PJ76, September 17, 2025**; a second pass said the final visible rows were PJ67 (2024 Oct 23) through **PJ76, 2025 Aug 15**. Because of this inconsistency (a known limitation of the automated page-summarization step, not a source disagreement), I mark **the exact PJ76 date as UNVERIFIED** (probably in Aug–Sept 2025, both extractions agree on that window and on "76" as the number). Separately, a search snippet referenced "Perijove 71" safe-mode events around April 2025 and a next perijove around May 7, 2025 including a distant (~55,300 mile) Io pass — consistent with, but not pinned precisely against, the table. Given confirmed spacecraft activity into May 2026 (Thebe flyby) on an approximately 33-day cadence since Sept 2025, the true current perijove count by Sept 2026 is almost certainly well above 76 (rough arithmetic: ~12 months / 33 days ≈ 11 more orbits ⇒ order PJ87–90), but I could not confirm an exact current number from a directly-fetched, internally-consistent authoritative table. **UNVERIFIED — recommend the researcher load https://www.missionjuno.swri.edu/mission-perijoves directly in a browser to read the current last row.**
- Bottom line: Juno was **not confirmed ended**; best evidence says it continued operating into at least May 2026 on congressionally-restored funding, with its official extended-mission authorization status ambiguous/uncertain as of the most recent sources found (March 2026).

## Q4 — JIRAM imager/spectrometer facts (PRIMARY SOURCE: Adriani et al. 2017, read directly)

Direct quotes from the paper (S6, p. 9 of the article, section 3.1):

- **IFOV:** *"each channel has a Field of View (FOV) of 1.75°x5.94° (128x432 pixels ... ) and an Instantaneous Field of View (IFOV) of about 250x250 μrad."* → **~250×250 microradians** for the imager; the spectrometer slit IFOV is separately given as **250 μrad**. (Secondary PDS page S7 rounds this to "about 240 μrad" — minor rounding discrepancy; primary paper's ~250 μrad is authoritative.) VERIFIED, primary source.
- **Frame/detector size:** *"Both the imager and the spectrometer channels use Hybrid Thinned HgCdTe photodiodes on CMOS ROIC focal planes with the same characteristics: 270x438 pixels arrays, 40x40 μm pixel pitch."* The imager focal plane is *"divided in two equal areas by the superimposition of two different band-pass filters"* — each band's usable image is **128×432 pixels**, so the two bands together span **256×432 pixels** (i.e., 432×256 as posed in the question, split between L and M halves), separated by *"an opaque region of dimension equivalent to about 10 pixels."* VERIFIED, primary source.
- **L-band:** *"filter L, centered at 3.455 μm with a 290 nm bandwidth"*; *"The L-channel is designed to obtain high resolution images of the polar auroras by selecting the H3+ emissions."* → L-band sees **H3+ auroral emission**, not reflected sunlight. VERIFIED, primary source.
- **M-band:** *"filter M, centered at 4.780 μm with a 480 nm bandwidth"*; *"the M-channel is suited to image the deeper atmosphere, in particular hot spots."* Elsewhere the paper explains hot spots are windows where thermal radiation escapes from ~5–6 bar depth through gaps in the ammonia cloud deck (p. 4: *"hot spots are windows into the deep atmosphere and allow to probe the properties of the atmosphere down to 5-6 bar in the 4-5 μm low opacity spectral region"*), i.e., **clear/thin-cloud regions appear bright (more thermal flux escaping), thick clouds appear dark** — consistent with the question's framing. VERIFIED, primary source, M-band central wavelength = **4.78 μm exactly as hinted**.
- **Spectrometer slit:** *"a slit 256 samples-wide with a FOV of 3.52° and an IFOV of 250 μrad ... the instantaneous acquisition of the detector is made of 336 images of the slit taken along the detector's columns in the spectral range 2-5 μm with a spectral sampling of about 8.99 nm/band."* → **256 pixels, 2–5 μm range, 336 spectral channels**, ~8.99 nm/band sampling. VERIFIED, primary source, matches question's hinted numbers exactly.
- **Spacecraft spin (also confirms Q5):** *"Juno is a 2 rpm (12 deg/sec) spinning solar-powered spacecraft."* VERIFIED, primary source.

## Q5 — Observing geometry

- **Perijove altitude:** Sources disagree modestly on the "typical" figure — S17 (JPL press kit): *"the spacecraft's point of closest approach above the cloud tops varies with each flyby — coming as close as about 2,600 miles (4,200 kilometers) and as far out as 4,900 miles (7,900 kilometers)"* — so **~4,200–7,900 km range**, with PJ1 at 4,200 km. S3 separately cites *"2,200 miles (3,500 kilometers)"* for an unspecified (possibly closest-ever) pass. The question's guess of "~4,000 km" is in the right ballpark (closest end of the range) but not a single fixed value. VERIFIED as a range, not a single number.
- **Distance from poles during a pass:** Not found in a directly-fetched, quotable primary source in this pass. The question's "order 100,000 km" figure is plausible (consistent with a highly elliptical polar orbit where perijove altitude is a few thousand km but the spacecraft is much farther from the planet when it crosses high latitudes on approach/departure) but **UNVERIFIED** — no source found and quoted with this specific number.
- **North-to-south pole pass duration:** S16 (JPL image caption, PIA21645): *"In just two hours, the spacecraft travels from a perch over Jupiter's north pole through its closest approach (perijove), then passes over the south pole."* → **~2 hours**, VERIFIED, matches question's hint exactly.
- **Longitude shift per orbit:** Could not pin down a single authoritative "degrees per orbit" figure via direct fetch. WebSearch-synthesized snippets (S18, not independently re-fetched/quote-verified) described an original 14-day-orbit plan with 90°, then 24°, spacing between successive perijoves, plus small trim maneuvers after each pass to walk the ground-track. Because the mission actually flew ~53-day (later 43/38/33-day) orbits rather than the original 14-day plan, this specific degree figure is **UNVERIFIED** for the orbit period actually flown — flagged as an open question rather than reported as fact.
- **Spacecraft spin period:** **2 rpm (12°/s)**, i.e., **30 seconds per rotation**. VERIFIED directly from the Adriani et al. 2017 primary paper (S6): *"Juno is a 2 rpm (12 deg/sec) spinning solar-powered spacecraft."* This matches the question's "30 s, 2 rpm" hint exactly.

## Uncertainties
1. **Current perijove count (Sept 2026):** Not pinned down. The SwRI perijove table is the right authoritative source but two automated fetches of it returned inconsistent last-row data; independent evidence (Thebe flyby May 1, 2026) shows the mission was still flying ~7+ months after the "official" Sept 2025 extension end, so the true count is almost certainly well past PJ76, plausibly ~PJ85–90, but this is an estimate, not a confirmed figure.
2. **Whether a formal 3rd mission extension was ever approved by NASA**, versus the mission simply continuing on congressionally-restored appropriations without a new named "extended mission" milestone like the Jan-2021 announcement — genuinely unclear from sources found; as of the latest directly-fetched status article (AIAA, March 24, 2026), NASA had not announced a decision either way.
3. **Perijove-to-perijove longitude shift** in degrees under the orbit periods Juno actually flew (53/43/38/33 days) was not found in a directly fetched, quotable primary source — the only degree figures found describe the abandoned pre-launch 14-day-orbit plan.
4. **Typical distance from the poles during a pass** (~100,000 km, per the question's own hint) — plausible given the highly eccentric polar orbit geometry, but not found in any source fetched in this pass.
5. Minor date discrepancies between sources (e.g., Ganymede flyby "June 7" vs "June 8," 2021; JOI "July 4" US date vs "July 5" UTC) are most likely time-zone/UTC-vs-local artifacts rather than real disagreements, but were not resolved to the second.
